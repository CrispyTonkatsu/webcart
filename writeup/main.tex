\section{Deriving Equations of Motion}
\subsection{Variables}
Simplifying the problem by treating it as 2 point masses where $c$ is
the point on the cart and then $p$ is at the other end of the pole.
The masses are at a distance $l$ of each other.

The problem needs 2 generalized variables:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item The horizontal displacement of the cart: $x$
	\item The angle to the up axis of the pole: $\theta$
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Kinematics}
Finding the position of the mass at the end of the pole $(x_p, y_p)$:

\begin{equation}
	x_p = x - l \sin(\theta)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
	y_p = l \cos(\theta)
\end{equation}

Now finding the first order derivative for the kinetic speed:

\begin{equation}
	\dot{x_p} = \dot{x} - l \dot{\theta} \cos(\theta)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
	\dot{y_p} = -l \dot{\theta} \sin(\theta)
\end{equation}

\subsection{Lagrangian}
The kinetic energy for the cart is:
\begin{align*}
	T & = \frac{1}{2}m_c \dot{x}^2 + \frac{1}{2}m_p (\dot{x_p}^2 + \dot{y_p}^2) \\
	T & = \frac{1}{2}m_c \dot{x}^2
	+ \frac{1}{2}m_p (
	\dot{x}^2 - 2\dot{x}l\dot{\theta}\cos(\theta) + l^2 \dot{\theta}^2 \cos^2(\theta)
	+ l^2 \dot{\theta}^2 \sin^2 (\theta)
	)
\end{align*}

Simplifying with trigonometric identities:
\begin{equation}
	T = \frac{1}{2}m_c \dot{x}^2
	+ \frac{1}{2}m_p (
	\dot{x}^2 - 2\dot{x}l\dot{\theta}\cos(\theta) + l^2 \dot{\theta}^2
	)
\end{equation}

The potential energy for the cart is:
\begin{align*}
	U & = mgy_p
\end{align*}

\begin{equation}
	U = m_pgl\cos(\theta)
\end{equation}

This leads to the final Lagrangian of:
\begin{align*}
	L & = T - U                    \\
	L & = \frac{1}{2}m_c \dot{x}^2
	+ \frac{1}{2}m_p (
	\dot{x}^2 - 2\dot{x}l\dot{\theta}\cos(\theta) + l^2 \dot{\theta}^2
	)
	- m_pgl\cos(\theta)
\end{align*}

Which simplifies to the following:
\begin{equation}
	L = \frac{1}{2}(m_c + m_p)\dot{x}^2
	- m_p\dot{x}l\dot{\theta}\cos(\theta) + \frac{1}{2} m_p l^2 \dot{\theta}^2
	- m_pgl\cos(\theta)
\end{equation}

\subsection{Euler Lagrange Equation}
To optimize the energy ledger we have made, we can now use the Euler-Lagrange
equation to get the final equation of motion for both $x$ and $\theta$.

With respect to $x$:
$$
	\frac{d}{dt} \pfrac{L}{\dot{x}} - \pfrac{L}{x} = \mathbf{F}(t)
$$

\begin{align}
	\pfrac{L}{\dot{x}}              & = (m_c + m_p)\dot{x} - m_p l \dot{\theta}\cos(\theta)                                    \\
	\frac{d}{dt} \pfrac{L}{\dot{x}} & = (m_c + m_p)\ddot{x} - m_p l \ddot{\theta}\cos(\theta) + m_pl\dot{\theta}^2\sin(\theta) \\
	\pfrac{L}{x}                    & = 0
\end{align}

\begin{equation}
	(m_c + m_p)\ddot{x} - m_pl\ddot{\theta}\cos(\theta) + m_pl\dot{\theta}^2\sin(\theta) = \mathbf{F}(t)
\end{equation}

With respect to $\theta$:
$$
	\frac{d}{dt} \pfrac{L}{\dot{\theta}} - \pfrac{L}{\theta} = 0
$$

\begin{align}
	\pfrac{L}{\dot{\theta}}              & = -m_p\dot{x}l\cos(\theta) + m_p l^2 \dot{\theta}                                         \\
	\frac{d}{dt} \pfrac{L}{\dot{\theta}} & = -m_p\ddot{x}l\cos(\theta) + m_p\dot{x}l\dot{\theta}\sin(\theta) + m_p l^2 \ddot(\theta) \\
	\pfrac{L}{\theta}                    & = m_p\dot{x}l\dot{\theta}\sin(\theta) + m_p g l \sin{\theta}
\end{align}

\begin{equation} \label{ThetaMotion}
	-m_p\ddot{x}l\cos(\theta) + m_p\dot{x}l\dot{\theta}\sin(\theta) + m_p l^2 \ddot(\theta) + m_p\dot{x}l\dot{\theta}\sin(\theta) + m_p g l \sin{\theta} = 0
\end{equation}

Equation \eqref{ThetaMotion} can be simplified by dividing the mass out as it is not being influenced by any forces:
\begin{equation}
	-\ddot{x}l\cos(\theta) + \dot{x}l\dot{\theta}\sin(\theta) + l^2 \ddot(\theta) + \dot{x}l\dot{\theta}\sin(\theta) + g l \sin{\theta} = 0
\end{equation}

\subsection{Summary}
To summarize, the equations of motion with respect to displacement $x$ and angle $\theta$ are:

\begin{align}
	(m_c + m_p)\ddot{x} - m_pl\ddot{\theta}\cos(\theta) + m_pl\dot{\theta}^2\sin(\theta) = \mathbf{F}(t) \\
	-\ddot{x}l\cos(\theta) + \dot{x}l\dot{\theta}\sin(\theta) + l^2 \ddot(\theta) + \dot{x}l\dot{\theta}\sin(\theta) + g l \sin{\theta} = 0
\end{align}

\section{Simulating the pendulum}
With the equations of motion, the system is not trivial to simulate compared to
the Newtonian description of the system.

\subsection{Symplectic Euler Method}
This method is relatively simple to implement and provides enough stability
in the system. The discretized version of this method is defined as such:
\begin{align*}
	v_{n+1} = v_t + g(t_n,x_n) \Delta t
	x_{n+1} = x_t + g(t_n,v_{n+1}) \Delta t
\end{align*}

\subsection{Implementation details}
To simulate the model in a visual context, rust will be used due to its ease of
setup, ability to compile for native and wasm platforms. For collisions,
I'll be utilizing a brute force broad phase while using analytical solutions
to shpere-sphere collisions for a throwable ball that causes disturbances.

\section{Controlling the pendulum}
This problem is the fruit fly of control theory, as it is a simple
non-linear system that allows for applications of both classical
and modern control. For this problem we ideally want our pendulum to
be upright and also to stay bounded within a certain region.
